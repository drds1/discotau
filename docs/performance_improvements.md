# Performance improvements: theory and before/after analysis

This document covers five changes made together, what each one does
mathematically, and what it measurably bought:

1. **Precomputed trig matrices**: the lag-to-frequency transform and the
   frequency-to-time basis are built once per fit, not on every NUTS step.
2. **Dense NUTS mass matrix by default.**
3. **Exact linear-parameter marginalisation** (`EchoFit(marginalise_linear=True)`),
   including the float32 numerical trap it hit and the QR fix.
4. **A direct solve**, `EchoFit.optimise()`: L-BFGS on the marginalised
   posterior plus a Laplace approximation, with no MCMC.
5. **True blackbody colours** for the accretion-disk animation.

Every chart and number below is regenerated by
`scripts/plot_performance_analysis.py` (raw numbers in
[`images/perf/results.json`](images/perf/results.json)). All timings are
under `jax.jit` (see `CLAUDE.md` decision #9 for why eager timings
mislead), measured one run at a time on an otherwise idle CPU (macOS, JAX on
CPU), on one fixed synthetic dataset: 5 bands (u, g, r, i, z) × 100
observations, `n_freq = 60`, `n_tau = 400`, `M_BH = 10^8 M_sun`, true
`log_mdot_msun_yr = −1.753` (0.018 M☉/yr). Absolute timings depend on the machine; the ratios are
what carry over.

## Summary

| | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| One NUTS gradient evaluation (sampled model) | 8.32 ms | 0.28 ms | **30× faster** |
| `transfer_coeffs`, `n_tau = 400` | 0.68 ms | 0.026 ms | 26× faster |
| Default NUTS fit, 500 + 500 (diagonal → dense mass) | 127 s, min ESS 56 | 56 s, min ESS 413 | **17× more ESS per second** |
| Direct solve, `optimise()` (new) | n/a | 27 s, no MCMC | 2.1× faster than dense NUTS, same `log_mdot_msun_yr` |
| NUTS on the marginalised model (new, opt-in) | n/a | 134 s, min ESS 485 | *slower* than dense NUTS: kept opt-in |
| Marginal log-likelihood error in float32 (Cholesky → QR) | 0.022 | 0.00009 | 240× more accurate |

The posterior itself is unchanged throughout: every method gives
`log_mdot_msun_yr` = −1.760 to −1.757 ± 0.011 to 0.012 (truth −1.753).

Two results went against what was predicted before measuring, and the
document keeps them:

- **Marginalising the linear parameters does not speed up NUTS** once each
  sampled step is cheap and the mass matrix is dense (section 3). Its
  payoff is the direct solve (section 4).
- **The direct solve is not "seconds" end to end.** L-BFGS itself takes
  about 5 s; most of the 27 s total is one-off JIT compilation of the
  Hessian and the posterior draws.

## 0. The model in one paragraph

The driver is a Fourier series on a fixed frequency grid $\omega_k$,

```math
X(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{n_f} \big[S_k \sin(\omega_k t) + C_k \cos(\omega_k t)\big],
```

and band $b$ sees it through a causal response $\psi_b(\tau)$ that depends on
the nonlinear disk parameters (`log_mdot_msun_yr`, inclination), scaled and offset:

```math
y_b(t) = S_b \int_0^\infty \psi_b(\tau)\, X(t - \tau)\, d\tau + C_b + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2).
```

Because $X$ is a sum of sinusoids, the convolution is closed-form
(`CLAUDE.md` decision #2). With the response's Fourier coefficients

```math
A_k = \int_0^\infty \psi(\tau)\cos(\omega_k\tau)\,d\tau, \qquad B_k = \int_0^\infty \psi(\tau)\sin(\omega_k\tau)\,d\tau,
```

the echo is

```math
\int \psi(\tau) X(t-\tau)\,d\tau = \sum_k \big[\sin(\omega_k t)(S_k A_k + C_k B_k) + \cos(\omega_k t)(C_k A_k - S_k B_k)\big].
```

The sampled parameters are the nonlinear ones (about 8 to 10 of them) plus
$2 n_f$ Fourier coefficients (as unit-Normal `S_raw`, `C_raw`, scaled by the
power-spectrum prior, decision #1) plus one offset per band: 133 in total
for the benchmark dataset.

## 1. Precomputed trig matrices

### Theory

`transfer_coeffs` evaluates $A_k$ and $B_k$ by the trapezoidal rule on the
lag grid $\tau_j$, with weights $w_j$:

```math
A_k \approx \sum_{j=1}^{n_\tau} w_j\, \psi(\tau_j) \cos(\omega_k \tau_j) = (W_c\,\psi)_k, \qquad (W_c)_{kj} = w_j \cos(\omega_k\tau_j),
```

and likewise $B = W_s \psi$ with $(W_s)_{kj} = w_j\sin(\omega_k\tau_j)$. Both
$\omega_k$ and $\tau_j$ are fixed for the whole fit (built once by
`EchoFit.build_grid`). Only $\psi$ changes between NUTS steps. Yet the
original code rebuilt the $n_f \times n_\tau$ phase matrix and took its
cosine and sine on every call, for every band, on every leapfrog step, and
again in the backward pass. That is $2 n_f n_\tau = 48{,}000$ transcendental
evaluations per band per step, against a matrix-vector product of the same
size, which is far cheaper.

The same holds on the observation side. `compute_echo` needs
$\sin(\omega_k t_i)$ and $\cos(\omega_k t_i)$ at each band's fixed
observation times $t_i$, an $n_\text{obs} \times n_f$ basis that never
changes either.

So `EchoFit._model_kwargs()` now builds $W_c, W_s$ once
(`forward_model.transfer_matrices`, in float64 and then cast, so if
anything slightly more accurate than before) and each light curve's
$(\sin\omega t, \cos\omega t)$ basis once (`forward_model.fourier_basis`).
The model uses them via optional arguments. With the arguments omitted,
every function computes on the fly exactly as before, so plotting and
standalone calls are unchanged.

### Why not the CREAM Fortran's early cut-off?

The original prompt for this work was porting the CREAM Fortran code's
`itaumax` optimisation: stop the convolution lookback at the first lag where
$\psi$ has decayed back to zero. That was a real saving there, because CREAM
convolves in *real space*: an $(n_t \times n_\tau)$ double loop per band,
where every skipped lag saves $n_t$ multiply-adds. In discotau the lag grid
only enters through $W_c\psi$ and $W_s\psi$. After precomputation, the
whole lag-dependent cost is one ~0.03 ms matrix-vector product, so a
cut-off has almost nothing left to save. It also could not be dynamic:
`jax.jit` compiles fixed array shapes, so a $\psi$-dependent cut-off would
still multiply the same number of zeros.

### Results

Transfer-coefficient cost against lag-grid size. The rebuilt version grows
linearly with `n_tau` (it is transcendental-bound); the precomputed one is
flat at about 0.03 ms, dominated by call overhead, not arithmetic:

![transfer_coeffs cost vs n_tau](images/perf/transfer_scaling.png)

| `n_tau` | 100 | 200 | 400 | 800 | 1600 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rebuilt every call (ms) | 0.20 | 0.37 | 0.68 | 1.30 | 2.50 |
| Precomputed (ms) | 0.031 | 0.028 | 0.026 | 0.029 | 0.030 |
| Speed-up | 6× | 13× | 26× | 45× | 83× |

In the whole model, with 5 bands and both the transfer matrices and the
observation-time bases precomputed, one potential-plus-gradient evaluation
(one NUTS leapfrog step) drops from **8.32 ms to 0.28 ms**, a 30× speed-up
with an identical result (`tests/test_precomputed_transfer.py` checks value
and gradient agree to float32 precision):

![Cost of one NUTS leapfrog step, three model variants](images/perf/gradient_cost.png)

The third bar, the marginalised model, is section 3's subject: fewer
dimensions, but each step costs 21× more than the precomputed sampled model.

## 2. Dense mass matrix by default

### Theory

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo simulates a particle with position $z$ (the
unconstrained parameters) and momentum $p$ in the potential
$U(z) = -\log p(z \mid y)$, with Hamiltonian

```math
H(z, p) = U(z) + \tfrac12\, p^\top M^{-1} p, \qquad p \sim \mathcal N(0, M).
```

The mass matrix $M$ sets how momentum maps onto movement. NUTS's warmup
estimates it from the posterior covariance seen so far: a **diagonal**
$M$ rescales each parameter independently, while a **dense** $M$ also
rotates the dynamics to follow correlations. If the posterior is a narrow,
tilted ridge, a diagonal $M$ forces a step size small enough for the
ridge's narrow width, so the trajectory needs very many steps to travel its
length. NUTS then hits `max_tree_depth` (1023 steps) before it ever makes a
U-turn.

This model has exactly such ridges. The clearest is the driver-amplitude and
band-gain degeneracy (`CLAUDE.md` decision #13): scaling the driver by
$\lambda$ and every $S_b$ by $1/\lambda$ leaves the likelihood unchanged,
so the posterior only pins down products. Decision #17 found that a
diagonal $M$ puts nearly every sample at the 1023-step ceiling, and made
`dense_mass` available as an option. With decision #1's steps now cheap,
the question became which setting gives more **effective samples per
second**. Effective sample size (ESS) discounts autocorrelated draws, so
ESS/s measures useful posterior information per unit of compute. The answer
was decisive, and `dense_mass=True` is now the default.

A dense $M$ has $O(P^2)$ entries to estimate during warmup instead of
$O(P)$, so it needs a reasonable `num_warmup` (the default 1000 is fine).
The README animation pins `dense_mass=False`, because its deliberately tiny
warmup can't adapt a dense matrix.

### Results

Same data, same seed, 500 warmup + 500 samples, sampled model with
precomputed matrices, diagonal against dense mass:

![Trajectory length per NUTS sample](images/perf/steps_per_sample.png)

With a diagonal mass matrix *every* sample hits the 1023-step ceiling. With
a dense one the median is 63 steps, and nothing gets near the ceiling.

| | Diagonal (old default) | Dense (new default) |
|---|---|---|
| Median leapfrog steps per sample | 1023 (ceiling) | 63 |
| Adapted step size | 0.0035 | 0.13 |
| Warmup / sampling time | 61 s / 67 s | 46 s / 10 s |
| ESS `log_mdot_msun_yr` / `cos_inclination` / `sigma_drw` / `S_g` | 228 / 356 / 69 / 56 | 504 / 413 / 632 / 700 |
| Min ESS per sampling second | 0.84 | **39.4** (47×) |
| Min ESS per total second | 0.44 | **7.4** (17×) |
| Divergences | 0 | 0 |
| `log_mdot_msun_yr` | −1.757 ± 0.011 | −1.760 ± 0.011 |

![ESS per sampling second, per parameter](images/perf/ess_per_second.png)

The gain is largest on exactly the parameters caught in the degeneracy
ridge (`sigma_drw` and the band gain `S_g`: ~60× and ~80×), as the theory
predicts.

## 3. Exact linear-parameter marginalisation

### Theory

Hold the nonlinear parameters fixed. Then every prediction is **linear**
in the $2n_f$ Fourier coefficients and in the offsets $C_b$. Collect those
into a vector $\theta$ in prior-whitened form: `S_raw`, `C_raw` are already
$\mathcal N(0,1)$, and each offset is written as $5\mkern3mu u_b$ with
$u_b \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$, matching its $\mathcal N(0, 5^2)$ prior. Stacking
every observation of every band (and a driver light curve, if one is
registered):

```math
y = M\theta + \varepsilon, \qquad \theta \sim \mathcal N(0, I_p), \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, D), \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_i^2).
```

Row $i$ of $M$, for an observation of band $b$ at time $t_i$, is

```math
M_{i,\cdot} = \Big[\, S_b\, s_k\big(\sin(\omega_k t_i)A_{bk} - \cos(\omega_k t_i)B_{bk}\big)_k,\;\; S_b\, s_k\big(\sin(\omega_k t_i)B_{bk} + \cos(\omega_k t_i)A_{bk}\big)_k,\;\; 5\,e_b \,\Big],
```

where $s_k$ is the power-spectrum prior scale and $e_b$ the band's indicator
vector. $M$ depends on the nonlinear parameters only, through $A$, $B$,
$S_b$ and $s_k$.

A linear-Gaussian model integrates out exactly:

```math
y \mid \text{nonlinear} \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big(0,\; \Sigma\big), \qquad \Sigma = D + M M^\top.
```

$\Sigma$ is $N \times N$ (here $N = 500$), but $MM^\top$ has rank only
$p = 2n_f + n_\text{offsets}$ (here 125). Write $\tilde M = D^{-1/2}M$,
$\tilde y = D^{-1/2}y$ and $P = I_p + \tilde M^\top \tilde M$. Then the matrix
determinant lemma and the Woodbury identity reduce everything to
$p \times p$ algebra:

```math
\log|\Sigma| = \log|D| + \log|P|,
```

```math
y^\top \Sigma^{-1} y \;=\; \min_\theta \Big[\, \lVert \tilde y - \tilde M\theta \rVert^2 + \lVert\theta\rVert^2 \Big] \;=\; \lVert \tilde y - \tilde M\hat\theta\rVert^2 + \lVert\hat\theta\rVert^2, \qquad \hat\theta = P^{-1}\tilde M^\top \tilde y.
```

(The middle equality is the completed square: minimising the joint
$-\log p(y, \theta)$ over $\theta$ leaves exactly the marginal's quadratic
form.) The marginal log-likelihood added to the model, as a single
`numpyro.factor`, is

```math
\log p(y \mid \text{nonlinear}) = -\tfrac12\Big[\lVert \tilde y - \tilde M\hat\theta\rVert^2 + \lVert\hat\theta\rVert^2 + \log|P| + \log|D| + N\log 2\pi\Big].
```

NUTS now samples only the ~8 nonlinear parameters. The linear ones are not
lost: their conditional posterior is exactly

```math
\theta \mid y, \text{nonlinear} \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big(\hat\theta,\; P^{-1}\big),
```

so after the fit, `EchoFit._add_linear_draws` draws one $\theta$ per
posterior sample and stores it under the usual site names (`S_raw`, `C_raw`,
`S`, `C`, `C_{band}`, `y_pred_*`). Drawing
$\theta_s \sim p(\theta \mid y, z_s)$ for each $z_s \sim p(z \mid y)$ samples
the exact joint posterior $p(z, \theta \mid y)$, so every plot, including the
driver's credible envelope, works unchanged and means the same thing as
before. The test suite checks the marginal likelihood against brute-force
float64 integration of the default model (using `jax.jacobian` of its own
predictions as an independent route to $M$) to about $10^{-5}$ relative.

### The numerical trap: float32, conditioning, and why QR

The first implementation took a Cholesky factor $P = LL^\top$. It was
correct in float64 and badly wrong in float32 (JAX's default). $P$'s
condition number on the benchmark is

```math
\kappa(P) \approx 3 \times 10^{7},
```

because the data pin some directions of $\theta$ down very tightly (hundreds
of points at 5% errors) while the prior alone controls others. Cholesky's
relative error grows like $\kappa(P)\mkern3mu \epsilon_{32}$, with
$\epsilon_{32} \approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$, so it is of order one here. The
log-likelihood *value* came out only slightly wrong (~0.02). The *gradients*
were ~9% off, and noisy gradients are what HMC cannot tolerate: energy
errors made NUTS shrink its step size, and the "8-dimensional" posterior
needed a median of 143 to 207 leapfrog steps per sample. The same fit in
float64 needed 7.

Two fixes were considered:

- **Global float64** (`jax_enable_x64`): exact, but process-wide. A library
  shouldn't flip it on for its user.
- **QR of the stacked least-squares system**, adopted (below).

Minimising $\lVert\tilde y - \tilde M\theta\rVert^2 + \lVert\theta\rVert^2$
is ordinary least squares on

```math
\begin{bmatrix}\tilde M \\ I_p\end{bmatrix}\theta \approx \begin{bmatrix}\tilde y \\ 0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix}\tilde M \\ I_p\end{bmatrix} = QR,
```

with $R^\top R = P$, so $\log|P| = 2\sum_i \log|R_{ii}|$,
$\hat\theta = R^{-1} Q_{1:N}^\top\tilde y$, and a conditional draw is
$\theta = \hat\theta + R^{-1}\eta$, $\eta \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$. The key
property: $\kappa(R) = \sqrt{\kappa(P)} \approx 5800$. QR never forms $P$,
so it never squares the conditioning.

Separately, the quadratic form is always evaluated as the residual at the
minimiser, never as the textbook $\tilde y^\top\tilde y - b^\top P^{-1}b$
(with $b = \tilde M^\top\tilde y$). The textbook form subtracts two large,
nearly equal numbers and loses the answer to cancellation. The residual
form is a minimum, so an error in $\hat\theta$ only perturbs it at second
order. Jacobi (diagonal) preconditioning of $P$ was also tried; it only
reduced $\kappa$ about threefold and didn't help.

### Results: accuracy

The benchmark's own linear system ($N = 500$ observations, $p = 125$ linear
parameters, $\kappa(P) = 3.4 \times 10^7$), at one fixed set of nonlinear
parameters, evaluated three ways in float32 against a float64 reference
($\log p = -3615.295$):

| float32 method | Log-likelihood error | Gradient differs from QR's by | Cost (value + gradient) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cholesky, textbook quadratic form | 0.145 | 15.7% | 1.2 ms |
| Cholesky, residual quadratic form | 0.022 | 4.9% | 1.2 ms |
| **QR, residual form (implemented)** | **0.00009** | reference | 4.0 ms |

And the consequence for NUTS, from the development runs (dense mass, 300 +
300) that exposed the problem:

| Marginal likelihood via | Adapted step size | Median steps per sample |
|---|---|---|
| float32 Cholesky | tiny | 143 to 207 |
| float64 Cholesky | 0.43 | 7 |
| **float32 QR** | 0.44 | **7** |

### Results: speed

The QR costs 3.4× a Cholesky, and the marginalised model's step (5.76 ms
including everything else) costs 21× the precomputed sampled model's
(0.28 ms). It needs about 9× fewer steps (median 7 against 63), and its
draws are slightly less autocorrelated, but that is not enough:

![Time to a posterior, all four methods](images/perf/time_to_posterior.png)

| | NUTS, sampled, dense (default) | NUTS, marginalised, dense |
|---|---|---|
| Sampled dimensions | 133 | 8 |
| Median leapfrog steps per sample | 63 | 7 |
| Adapted step size | 0.13 | 0.53 |
| Warmup / sampling time | 46 s / 10 s | 109 s / 25 s |
| Warmup leapfrog steps | 271,932 | 17,788 |
| ESS `log_mdot_msun_yr` / `cos_inclination` / `sigma_drw` / `S_g` | 504 / 413 / 632 / 700 | 1075 / 568 / 485 / 613 |
| Min ESS per sampling second | **39.4** | 19.1 |
| Min ESS per total second | **7.4** | 3.6 |

So `marginalise_linear` stays **opt-in and off by default**. It is exact,
and may win where the sampled model's geometry is much harder than here
(many more Fourier modes, or a mass matrix that can't adapt), but on this
benchmark plain dense sampling is about 2× more efficient. Section 1's
precomputation is what moved the goalposts: before it, each sampled step
cost 8.3 ms, more than a marginalised one.

## 4. The direct solve: `EchoFit.optimise()`

### Theory

With the linear parameters integrated out, the posterior lives in about 8
dimensions, few enough to *solve* rather than sample. Write
$U(z) = -\log p(z \mid y)$ in NumPyro's unconstrained space $z$ (positive
parameters as logs, bounded ones through logits, including the Jacobians).
`optimise()`:

1. **Finds the peak** $z^\star = \arg\min_z U(z)$ with L-BFGS
   (`scipy.optimize.minimize`, JAX-computed gradients), from the
   data-anchored initial point plus a few random restarts, keeping the best,
   then **polishes it with Newton steps** $z \leftarrow z - \alpha H^{-1}\nabla U$
   using the exact Hessian, backtracking $\alpha$ until $U$ falls and
   accepting a step only if it does, until the step is under 0.01 posterior
   standard deviations.
2. **Fits a Gaussian to the peak** (the Laplace approximation). A
   second-order expansion $U(z) \approx U(z^\star) + \tfrac12 (z-z^\star)^\top H (z-z^\star)$
   with $H = \nabla^2 U(z^\star)$ (exact, via `jax.hessian`) gives
   $p(z \mid y) \approx \mathcal N(z^\star, H^{-1})$.
3. **Draws** $z_s \sim \mathcal N(z^\star, H^{-1})$, maps them back to
   constrained parameters, and draws the linear parameters exactly for each
   (as in section 3).

It always uses the marginalised model. Maximising the *joint* posterior over
the ~130 Fourier coefficients as well would find a different point: the
joint mode ignores how much volume the conditional posterior of $\theta$
occupies, which is exactly the $-\tfrac12\log|P|$ term the marginal
includes. For scale hyperparameters such as `sigma_drw` that omission is a
known source of bias (in the centred parameterisation the joint density
even diverges as `sigma_drw` → 0, the funnel of decision #1).
Marginalising first is what makes a point estimate plus curvature a good
summary of the remaining parameters.

The Laplace approximation is exact when the posterior is Gaussian in $z$,
and good when it is close. It is wrong for multimodal posteriors, such as
`lag_mode="free"` bands with a broad lag prior (see `CLAUDE.md`'s rough
edges), and it underplays heavy tails. For those, `.fit()` remains the tool.
`.fit(init_from_optimum=True)` combines the two: it starts NUTS at $z^\star$,
with the linear parameters at their conditional mean.

### Results

One `optimise()` call, first in the process (so including compilation), with
4 L-BFGS starts and 1000 posterior draws:

| Stage | Time |
|---|---|
| L-BFGS, 4 starts (274 evaluations; 77 for the best start) | 4.9 s |
| Exact Hessian and Newton polishing (2 iterations; mostly compilation) | 8.3 s |
| 1000 Laplace draws plus exact linear draws (a batched QR each) | 13.0 s |
| Set-up (model initialisation, compilation) | ~1 s |
| **Total** | **27.1 s** |

That is 2.1× faster than a 500 + 500 dense NUTS run (56 s) on the same data.
Most of it is fixed overhead, so the gap widens for longer NUTS runs.

**Why the Newton polishing matters.** L-BFGS on its own can stop well short
of the peak. On the real NGC 5548 light curves (4 bands) it stopped 6.5
units of potential above the optimum, with the implied Newton step
$H^{-1}\nabla U$ 3.5 posterior standard deviations long, because it crawls
along the flat driver-amplitude/band-gain ridge (all four band gains had
the same, prior-set posterior width). Two line-searched Newton steps reached
the optimum exactly. On the benchmark it also took 2 steps (the residual
offset was 0.17 standard deviations beforehand, 0.00 after). Convergence is
judged on that posterior scale rather than by L-BFGS's own flag, whose line
search on a float32 objective often stops "abnormally" right at the optimum.
`optimise()` warns if the final offset exceeds 0.25.

![Posterior agreement across all four methods](images/perf/posterior_agreement.png)

| Posterior mean ± sd | `log_mdot_msun_yr` | inclination (°) | `sigma_drw` | `S_g` | `C_g` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NUTS, diagonal | −1.7576 ± 0.0108 | 60.0 ± 12.7 | 0.084 ± 0.037 | 0.64 ± 0.30 | −0.0007 ± 0.0137 |
| NUTS, dense | −1.7599 ± 0.0107 | 59.0 ± 13.5 | 0.079 ± 0.035 | 0.68 ± 0.30 | −0.0015 ± 0.0131 |
| NUTS, marginalised | −1.7589 ± 0.0115 | 60.7 ± 13.5 | 0.084 ± 0.039 | 0.65 ± 0.30 | −0.0020 ± 0.0139 |
| **`optimise()` (Laplace)** | **−1.7588 ± 0.0116** | **55.3 ± 14.0** | **0.082 ± 0.036** | **0.66 ± 0.30** | **−0.0011 ± 0.0132** |

Every parameter agrees to well within its posterior width. The visible
difference is inclination's *shape*: the true posterior rises towards and
piles up against the 80° prior bound, while the Laplace Gaussian (built in
NumPyro's logit space for the bounded `cos_inclination`) rolls off before
it. Its mean (55°) is centred on the posterior *mode*, a few degrees below
NUTS's mean (59°), as expected for a skewed parameter. That is the Laplace approximation's textbook limit, and why `.fit()` is
still the right tool when a parameter presses against a bound or the
posterior may be multimodal.

never optimised or sampled (they are exact conditional draws), matches full
MCMC almost line for line, envelope included:

## 5. Blackbody colours for the disk animation

The README animation's face-on disk used an arbitrary colour map. It now
shows each ring in the colour a blackbody at that ring's temperature
actually appears to the eye.

**Disk temperature.** The Shakura–Sunyaev viscous profile
(`forward_model.disk_temperature_profile`) is

```math
T(r) = T_\text{ref}\left(\frac{r}{r_\text{ref}}\right)^{-3/4}\left(\frac{1 - \sqrt{r_\text{in}/r}}{1 - \sqrt{r_\text{in}/r_\text{ref}}}\right)^{1/4},
```

anchored so $T(r_\text{ref}) = b/\lambda_\text{ref}$ (Wien's law) at the
lag-scaling reference radius. The second factor, the zero-torque inner
boundary, sends $T \to 0$ at the ISCO $r_\text{in}$ itself.

**Temperature to colour** (`plotting.blackbody_to_colour`):

1. Planck's law, up to a constant (normalised away later):
   $B_\lambda(T) \propto \lambda^{-5}\big/\big(e^{hc/\lambda k T} - 1\big)$.
2. Integrate against the CIE 1931 2° colour-matching functions (the
   analytic multi-lobe fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley 2013) over
   380–780 nm to get tristimulus values:
   $X = \int B_\lambda \bar x\mkern3mu d\lambda$, and likewise $Y$, $Z$.
3. Convert to linear sRGB (D65 white point) with the standard matrix
   (below), clip negatives, and divide by the largest channel. That keeps
   chromaticity only: a real disk's inner rings are also vastly brighter,
   which would saturate any display.
4. Apply sRGB gamma encoding.

The matrix in step 3:

```math
\begin{bmatrix}R\\G\\B\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.2406 & -1.5372 & -0.4986\\ -0.9689 & 1.8758 & 0.0415\\ 0.0557 & -0.2040 & 1.0570\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{bmatrix}
```

The result runs from deep orange-red at 1000–1500 K, through white near
6500 K (the D65 white point, as it should be), to pale blue above
$10^4$ K. The animation uses one fixed log-temperature colour scale for
every frame, floored at 1000 K, below which a blackbody barely glows in the
visible. Without that floor the ISCO's $T \to 0$ reached ~10 K, and the
Planck integral underflowed to NaN colours. Rings are coloured at their
midpoint temperature on a geometric radial grid, so the small hot core is
resolved while the face-on geometry stays linear in radius.

![Blackbody colour vs temperature, and a disk temperature profile drawn in its true colours](images/perf/blackbody_colours.png)

The hot, blue zone is genuinely small: $T > 10^4$ K only inside about a fifth
of the Wien radius. Most of the disk's area is cool and orange-red, which is
what the animation now shows.
